PageBook

A central limit order book for Soroban with deterministic footprints: every ledger key an operation touches is computable at simulation time. This page is the short version of the architecture doc: the same data structures and processes, with pictures and worked numbers.

Implementation status: the Rust contract, shared types, client padding helpers, and tests are in the repository. M0 through M3 are implemented, M4 resource hardening is partial, and the M5 client SDK sketch is present. A live status page reads the testnet market straight from RPC.

Why an order book is hard on Soroban 03, 07

Stellar already runs an order book: the classic DEX is part of the protocol. An offer is a ledger entry the protocol itself manages, and matching runs inside transaction apply, reading whatever offers it needs; one operation can cross up to 1,000 of them without naming any in advance. Holding an offer locks a refundable reserve rather than paying rent. An order book built as a Soroban contract instead works under two rules the classic DEX does not face.

The footprint is declared before execution. A transaction lists every ledger entry it will read or write; simulation computes the list, and the host rejects any access to an entry not on it. The classic DEX does not have this problem, because the protocol is the one doing the reading. A contract's matching walk has to name every entry it might touch before it runs, and the book keeps moving between simulation and inclusion. PageBook's answer is to make the footprint computable: every key is a pure function of identifiers the client already knows (market, side, tick, owner, nonce), and every loop bounds on a config constant, so a client can take one simulation, pad the key set against whatever the book does next, and submit a transaction that still fits.

Write bytes are the scarce shared resource. Each ledger, about five seconds, allows 286,720 bytes of Soroban writes, and Soroban charges for every byte of every entry a transaction rewrites, whether or not that byte changed. A classic offer is one small entry the protocol rewrites; a contract book that stores more than it must rewrites the excess on every place and every settle. A persistent entry also prepays about 120 days of storage as rent, and going too small does not help either, because every entry carries 100 to 200 bytes of key and framing on top of its payload. PageBook's answer is to keep every hot entry at a few hundred bytes and to pay for it once: the bulk-rewritten queues and bitmaps are packed bytes with tested size budgets, the smaller named entries are readable #[contracttype] structs, and long-lived entries are reused in place rather than deleted and recreated, with archival as the garbage collector.

Most of what follows is those two answers worked out.

The model in one paragraph 04 §0

One contract hosts many markets. A market is a pair of SAC addresses plus two numbers, lot_size and tick_size, that turn every quantity into an integer count of lots and every price into an integer tick. Each side of a market (bids, asks) is a set of price levels; each level is a FIFO queue of maker orders at exactly one price. A taker calls place, which crosses the book and settles in the same transaction. A maker's order rests, and later the maker calls settle, the one exit: it pays whatever happened, proceeds if the order filled, a refund if it did not, both if it partly filled. There is no rounding anywhere in matching, because lots × tick × tick_size is exact; the taker fee is the only place the contract rounds, and it rounds up.

Part I: data structures

There are nine PageBook entry types in three groups, plus the token contracts' vault balances. Two principles govern all of them: every key is a pure function of client-known identifiers, and the order store is authoritative while the tick index is derived.

Storage map 04 Part I

Keys are one #[contracttype] DataKey enum: the variant name is the type, the fields are coordinates. Nothing that execution assigns (a generation, a queue position, a page index) may appear in a key the client has to put in the footprint. All entries are persistent except Config. Sizes are XDR-serialized budgets at max occupancy, enforced by tests. Level and LevelPage use packed Bytes, as do the 2,048-bit TickWord and TickSummary bitmaps. Config, Market, BestTick, Order, and FeeAccrual use named #[contracttype] structs.

Plus the vault: the contract's own SAC balance entries, one per token. PageBook does not define them, but every settling transaction has them in its read-write footprint, which makes them the point where transactions serialize (the concurrency note under Budgets and fees).

The asymmetry between the two big groups is what lets the index be sloppy. The order store carries the hard rules (a Level is never deleted; nothing past the tail is ever read); the index only has to satisfy the one-directional staleness contract described with the tick index below, and is healed lazily by the next place that walks through.

The level queue 04 §2

A Level is one price's queue: five counters and a run of quantity slots. Queue position is by seq, and seq is implicit in slot position, so a slot stores only a quantity. Seqs beyond the inline slots spill into LevelPage entries. Nothing is ever moved or compacted; "remove the head" means a counter advanced.

Generations and capacity keep the queue sound over time. When matching empties a whole level, or the first rest arrives at a level that cancels emptied, generation increments and seqs restart at 0; every earlier order's settlement is unaffected, because an old generation number reads as "fully filled" (next section). And a page holds PAGE_SLOTS seqs; one generation holds at most INLINE_SLOTS + PAGE_SLOTS × MAX_PAGES orders, after which rests fail with LevelFull until the level empties and resets.

Order: the maker's claim 04 §3

A resting order is one Order entry. Its key is (market, owner, nonce), and the nonce is chosen by the client before submission. So the key is known at simulation time, whatever the book does before the transaction lands. The queue coordinates, which execution assigns, live inside the entry.

Keying by (generation, seq) would break under concurrency: any other rest at the same level moves the tail, any sweep bumps the generation, and the simulated key is wrong when the transaction executes.

The tick index 04 §5

Finding the next tick with liquidity is a two-level bitmap plus a cached best. TickSummary has one bit per TickWord; each TickWord has one bit per tick. Together they cover 2,048 × 2,048 ≈ 4.19 million ticks per side. BestTick is where the walk starts.

Part II: processes

Four mechanisms do the work (settlement, the matching walk, rest, and replace) and the entry points compose them. The client runs a fifth process off-chain: simulate, pad, submit.

Settlement 04 §7

Because every take at a level happens at exactly the tick price, it does not matter when a fill happened, only whether the queue moved past you. So an order's stored coordinates (g, s, q) compared with the level's counters (G, H, C) decide everything. Matching writes nothing per maker; the maker reads the answer off the counters when they settle.

The matching walk 04 §8

A taker's place starts at start_tick, the best opposite tick the client saw when it simulated, and walks toward its limit. Ticks better than start_tick are never visited: an order that arrived there after simulation is outside the footprint by construction, and the walk never reaches for it. Sweeping a whole level needs no slot reads (open_lots × tick × tick_size is exact) and is one small write: bump the generation. Only the last, partial level touches slots.

Rest, and replace 04 §9, §10

A rest is an append: seq = tail_seq++, write the quantity into that slot, write the Order, set the tick's bit if the level was empty, move BestTick if this is a new best, escrow with one SAC transfer. The slot must land inside the append window the client declared (the pages around the simulated tail); if a concurrent rest pushed the tail past it, the result is the typed error RetryRest, not a host trap on an undeclared key.

A maker updating a quote does not settle and place again. replace settles the old order per the table above, then rewrites the same Order entry with new coordinates and appends at the new tick, atomically. The entry keeps its key and its size, so no rent is charged, and the maker is never unquoted in between. replace_batch does this for up to MAX_REPLACE_BATCH quotes with one netted transfer per token.

The client's process 04 §14

Every key the contract touches is a function of (market, side, tick, word, page, owner, nonce), so the client builds the footprint itself. Its job is to pad that footprint so the transaction still fits the book it lands on, which may differ from the one simulateTransaction saw. Between simulation and inclusion, new levels can appear at any tick inside the walk range, and heads and tails can cross page boundaries.

Budgets and fees 04 §17

What comes out of all of the above: small hot entries, capped loops, and a client-padded footprint give a router a transaction it can simulate once and trust, and one that parallelizes with anything outside its read-write set. The numbers:

Matching is nearly free and placement pays rent: a 32-level sweep costs about half of resting one order, so the book's carrying cost sits with makers at roughly 0.046 XLM per open order per minimum TTL, which doubles as anti-spam for holding orders (churn is priced by min_order_lots: dust that arms a phantom best or poisons a queue gets filled or swept for its notional). And write bytes, not fees, are the throughput ceiling: the max sweep is 9.3% of the ledger-wide write-bytes limit (286,720), so a ledger fits about 10 of them, or hundreds of rests and settles. Every hot entry is a few hundred bytes for that reason.

Concurrency: the vault's SAC balance entries are the serialization points. Every settling transaction has one or both in its read-write footprint, so under P23 parallel execution all markets sharing a token form one dependent-transaction cluster. v1 accepts this because write bytes bind first; per-market vault sub-accounts are the v2 fix. No market operation writes the instance entry (no bump_instance on the hot path; keepalive() cranks the TTL), so instance reads stay read-only and never widen the cluster. 04 §16

Deferred to v2 or a sibling market type: global cross-market orders, per-market vault sub-accounts, permissionless market creation, geometric ticks, pooled pro-rata levels, volatility-scaled fees. Full list and reasons in 04 §20; the decisions behind the design in docs/decisions.
