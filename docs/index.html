PageBook · a Soroban-native CLOB

An order book designed around declared footprints

A Soroban transaction has to declare every ledger entry it touches before it runs. PageBook is built around that constraint. Every storage key is a pure function of identifiers the client already knows, every loop has a config-defined limit, and a client can compute and pad the full key set for an operation without reading chain state. A book that changes in flight cannot surprise the transaction into an undeclared key.

§ 1Why a CLOB is hard on Soroban

Three properties of Soroban rule out the usual order-book designs:

  • The footprint is declared before execution. Touch one undeclared ledger entry and the transaction fails. A matching engine that walks to wherever the next order happens to sit has an unpredictable footprint, and the book can change between simulation and inclusion.
  • Storage pays rent. Persistent entries have TTLs; when one lapses, the network archives the entry. A design that has to keep the whole book alive pays forever, while one that lets cold state sleep gets garbage collection for free.
  • Write bytes are the scarce shared resource. Each ledger allows about 286 KB of writes for all Soroban traffic, and every byte of every rewritten entry counts. Hot entries have to stay small, a few hundred bytes at most.

The design's answer: make every key computable off-chain, bound every loop with a config constant, and put every operation's worst case in a table.

§ 2The model

One contract hosts many markets. A market is a sorted SAC token pair plus quantization parameters, with a bid side and an ask side. Each side holds price levels, and a level is a FIFO queue of maker orders at a single price.

Takers cross the book and settle atomically in one transaction. Makers rest, then later settle through a single exit that pays whatever happened: a filled order pays proceeds, an open one refunds escrow, a partial fill pays both. Matching writes nothing per maker; three counters determine every maker's outcome later (§ 4).

Vocabulary, used consistently from here on Entry points are what you call: place (cross and/or rest an order), replace (move quotes), settle (the one maker exit), route, and the cranks. Behaviors are what a call did: take (consume resting liquidity), rest (leave an order on the book), sweep (consume a whole level) — one place may both take and rest. Roles follow behavior: the maker's order rests, the taker takes. Order states are open, partially filled, filled — "fill" is only ever an order state. Settling an open order cancels it; settling a filled order claims its proceeds. Cancel and claim name outcomes of settle, not operations.

Quantization: exact by construction

  • lot_size: base atoms per lot; quantities on the book are u64 lots.
  • tick_size: quote atoms per lot per tick. The price of tick t is t × tick_size, with t ≥ tick_min ≥ 1. Tick 0 would allow zero-price orders with no escrow, so it's excluded.
  • The quote value of any take is qty_lots × t × tick_size, an exact integer. There is no rounding anywhere in the matching path. The one exception in the whole system is the taker fee, ceil(output × fee_bps / 10⁴), whose dust accrues to the fee pot.
  • Overflow is proven impossible at market creation instead of checked per trade. With LEVEL_CAP as the maximum orders per level generation, creation enforces LEVEL_CAP × max_order_lots × tick_max × tick_size ≤ i128::MAX / (4 × MAX_ROUTE_LEGS), plus the same bound on the base side. That covers one order, a full level, a maximum sweep, and a full route, with headroom left for fee math. Creation also caps fee_bps at FEE_BPS_MAX. Orders outside [min_order_lots, max_order_lots] are rejected: the floor deters dust, and the ceiling completes the overflow proof.

§ 3Storage: every key a pure function

Every key derives from identifiers the client knows before submission: market, side, tick, word, page, owner, nonce. A client or SDK can compute all of them without reading chain state. Footprint padding depends on this, so the design treats it as non-negotiable.

A maker's handle is (owner, nonce), with the nonce chosen by the client before submission. That makes the OrderRef key known at simulation time, whatever the book does in flight. Execution assigns the queue coordinates and stores them inside the entry:

owner · noncekey: the client selects it before submission
side · tick · generation · seq · qtycontents: set at execution, given in the rested event

This split is load-bearing. Queue coordinates move under concurrency: any rest at the same level bumps tail_seq, and any sweep bumps generation, so a key built from them would be wrong by the time the transaction lands. The rule is simple: a key may only contain what the client knows before submission. The coordinates, known once the order rests, give the maker every key a settle will touch, the level plus at most one page.

Packed encoding is mandatory Hot entries (Level, Page) are fixed-layout Bytes blobs with a leading schema-version byte, not #[contracttype] structs. The map encoding inflates the payload by roughly 2.5x: a map-encoded Level at N=32 is about 1.2 KB instead of 384 B, an error that would flow into every write-byte and throughput figure downstream. Slots store only a quantity; the seq is implicit in the slot's position.

Escrow sits in the contract's own SAC balances, one balance entry per token. That entry is part of every settling footprint, and (as § 8 concedes) it is the real serialization point. Bids escrow quote; asks escrow base.

§ 4Three counters are a complete fill proof

A level's queue is positional and append-only. Within a generation, seq s lives in inline slot s when s < N, otherwise in page (s−N)/P. Slots never move or compact; removing the head means a counter advanced, nothing more. Three counters carry the entire fill history:

  • generation (G) increments whenever the queue resets, either swept empty by matching or reset by the first rest at a cancelled-empty level (below). Seqs restart at 0.
  • head_seq (H) is the first seq not yet fully filled.
  • head_consumed (C) counts lots taken from the head order. C stays strictly below the head's quantity; the moment it would reach it, H advances and C resets to 0.

Two lifecycle rules keep this sound over the long run. The empty-level reset: a rest that finds total_open = 0 with tail_seq > 0 resets the queue, bumping G and zeroing the counters. This is safe because every unclaimed order at that point is fully filled, and after the bump the g < G row pays it exactly the same amount. Without the rule, a level emptied by cancels drifts toward LevelFull and stays there. The stale-slot rule: page keys don't include the generation, so old slot data can linger under a live key. A slot only means something when its seq < tail_seq of the current generation, and readers ignore everything past that.

ConditionStateSettlement

That table is the entire settlement state machine: O(1), about three writes. Every take at a level happens at exactly the tick price, so it never matters when it happened. The counters are a complete proof.

Tombstone poisoning, and its bound Head advance skips tombstones, and an unbounded scan would invite an attack: rest K dust orders, cancel the middle ones, and every taker crossing that price pays an O(K) scan. A place therefore scans at most MAX_SLOTS_SCANNED slots, and head progress persists even when the cap ends the loop early, so the cleanup cost spreads across takers instead of repeating for each one. The per-market min_order_lots floor makes the attack expensive to set up in the first place.

§ 5Matching: a bounded walk over bitmaps

Price discovery is a two-tier bitmap per side. L1 summarizes 2,048 words and each L0 word covers 2,048 ticks, which gives 4.19 million ticks per side with the next set price always O(1) reads away. Sweeping a level clears its bit and walks on; the walk only ever reads bitmap words.

A sweep never reads the level's slots, since total_open × tick × tick_size is already exact. The only slot access in matching is partial consumption at the final level, and a client-declared page window bounds it, behaving exactly like a loop cap. The taker path, compressed: 

place(taker, mkt, side, limit_tick, qty, start_tick, nonce, window, flags):
  best = worse_of(Best(opposite), start_tick)   # never visit better ticks
  while qty > 0 and best crosses limit_tick
        and levels < MAX_LEVELS_CROSSED
        and slots_scanned < MAX_SLOTS_SCANNED:
    lvl = Level(opposite, best)
    if lvl.total_open == 0:        # stale bit → clear lazily, walk on
    if lvl.total_open <= qty:      # sweep: generation += 1, clear bit (no slot reads)
    else:                          # partial: advance head within declared window;
                                   #   window edge ⇒ graceful stop, like a cap
  if qty > 0:
    FoK ⇒ fail · post_only + crossed ⇒ fail
    cap- or window-terminated while still crossing ⇒ REFUND remainder
    else rest remainder (append must land in declared window, else RetryRest)
  settle two SAC transfers · fee = ceil(output × bps / 10⁴)
The book is never crossed A cap or window edge can stop the matching loop while the book still crosses the limit. When that happens, the contract refunds the remainder rather than resting it, because a rested remainder would put a bid above live asks and nothing downstream is defined on a crossed book. This is invariant 8.

The write side stays flat: a rest appends one quantity slot at tail_seq++, writes one OrderRef, and moves escrow in a single transfer. Post-only is conservative on purpose. It compares its tick against Best as stored, one read, and fails with Crossed even when that best is a stale bit over an emptied level. Since Best is never worse than the true best, the check may falsely reject near stale state but can never rest an order that actually crosses; hunting for the true best would either grow the footprint or cross the book. Top-of-book changes are events. Soroban can't cap the resources of a call into an untrusted contract, so there are no hooks anywhere. Routes share one levels-and-slots budget across all legs, which keeps a route's ceiling equal to a single maximum fill plus per-leg constants.

Makers update quotes with replace rather than settle+place. It settles the old order exactly per the settlement table, rewrites the same OrderRef in place with the new coordinates, and appends at the new tick. Reusing the entry at fixed size means zero rent, and the update is atomic, so the maker is never unquoted in between. replace_batch bounds a list of these at MAX_REPLACE_BATCH with settlement netted into one transfer per token: a full book refresh is one transaction. Replace never takes liquidity; it applies the same conservative post-only check and fails Crossed instead. Why this operation exists at all is a fee story, told in § 7.

§ 6Footprints: the product surface

Everything above exists so this section can work. A taker's client simulates the fill, learns which ticks it crosses, and then has to declare a footprint that stays valid while the book keeps moving. Three mechanisms get it there:

  • start_tick closes the better-priced direction. The fill begins at the simulated best and never visits anything better, so an order rested at a better price mid-flight simply isn't reachable. It can't cause an undeclared read and it can't fail the transaction. The late order keeps its place.
  • A contiguous band closes the worse direction. The client declares every Level key in [start_tick, pad_end], set or not; unset keys cost nothing beyond footprint slots. Padding only the currently-set ticks isn't safe, because a new level can appear at any tick in the range.
  • Slot windows close the queue races. A concurrent fill can move a level's head into overflow pages, and a concurrent rest can push a tail across a page boundary, keys the band doesn't cover. So the client declares small page windows and passes them as an argument: pages around the simulated head for each set level, and {page(tail), +1, page 0} for its own rest. Execution never reads past a window. Hitting the edge stops the loop like a cap, and an append that lands outside fails with the typed error RetryRest.
The complete list of failure modes A fill traps (a footprint violation) in exactly one case: the walk has to pass pad_end. Nothing else traps. The scan cap, the level cap, and the window edges all stop the loop with progress persisted and the remainder refunded, and a displaced append comes back as RetryRest. This list is the contract.

§ 7What operations cost

Estimates use live mainnet rates from August 2026: 7 stroops per 10k instructions, 2,500 per write entry, a 1,000-stroop-per-KB write and rent floor, 5,000 per KB of events (refundable), and about 4.4 stroops per transaction byte. The number that dominates is rent: roughly 1,667 stroops (0.000167 XLM) per byte per 120-day minimum TTL on every newly created persistent entry. Live-state reads are free after P23, so a declared key that never gets touched costs about 300 stroops of transaction bytes and nothing else.

Matching is nearly free; placement pays rent A 32-level sweep costs about the same as creating one OrderRef. The book's carrying cost sits with makers, about 0.027 XLM per open order per 120 days, an anti-spam floor that comes free with the storage model and stacks with min_order_lots: a K-order dust storm costs about 0.027 K XLM and none of it comes back. Level rent (about 0.064 XLM) is paid once per tick per 120 days of activity by whoever creates or restores the level. One caution: 1,000/KB is the protocol floor. The rate climbs toward 10,000/KB as live Soroban state approaches its 3 GB target, and every rent-dominated row scales with it.
Market making: churn is priced separately from holding On the classic Stellar DEX, makers re-center whole books every ledger or two because offer updates cost ~100 stroops and reserves come back. The settle+place path here re-pays OrderRef rent on every update (~0.031 XLM per quote), so a 40-order book refreshed every minute would burn ~1,800 XLM a day. That's why replace exists: with the entry reused, the same full refresh is one ~0.03 XLM transaction and per-quote carrying cost stays 0.000225 XLM/day at any update frequency. Capacity binds before fees do, though: at ~24 KB per refresh the network fits about 12 full-book refreshes per ledger, total. Makers here quote wider and re-center on threshold moves, not every ledger. Full analysis in docs/decisions/005.

§ 8Concurrency: the true limits

Bid and ask clusters do not parallelize, and markets are not disjoint under protocol 23. The vault's SAC balance entries are the real serialization points: one balance entry per (token, contract), read and written by every settling operation. Same-side rests serialize with each other, takers serialize with both sides, and every market sharing a token joins the same execution cluster.

§ 9Rent & archival: the archive is the garbage collector

Cold state isn't deleted; it lapses. A level far from the market's price stops being touched, its TTL runs out, and it sleeps in the archive costing nothing, counters intact. The next operation that needs it restores it, and whoever benefits pays the restore. That's the designed lifecycle, not a failure mode. Mainnet's minimum persistent TTL is now about 120 days, prepaid as rent at creation or restore, with a maximum near 180. As a result the policy is almost entirely passive: no hot path ever extends a TTL, and matching pays no rent on existing entries.

§ 10Administration and trust model

The contract custodies every maker's escrow, so the admin surface is narrow and deliberately asymmetric:

  • Initialization is the constructor. __constructor(admin, fee_recipient) runs at deploy; there is no init entry point and no first-caller race to steal admin.
  • Every state-changing entry point authenticates. Takers sign places and routes, owners sign settles and replaces, and the admin signs configuration, upgrades, pause, and market creation. The two cranks, collect_fees and keepalive, take no auth at all; their effects come from config, not from whoever calls them.
  • Pause blocks places and replaces. Settle always works, so funds can always leave, whatever the admin state.
  • The trust model: an admin that can upgrade the wasm can move the vault. Deployments holding real value should put the admin behind a multisig or timelock. A trustless deployment burns the admin and accepts that it can never upgrade.
  • Every hot entry carries a schema-version byte, and upgraded code migrates entries lazily as it touches them, so there's never a big-bang migration over live orders.
  • Caps follow the network. Validators retune Soroban's resource limits every few months, and a contract can't read that config (no host function exposes limits or remaining budget). So the per-market loop caps and order-size bounds are retunable through set_market_caps, an admin call that re-runs the creation overflow proof and refuses values that break it. Quantization and queue geometry stay frozen for a market's lifetime: slot positions and prices are pure functions of them, so changing them would corrupt live state. Client-side knobs (pad width, windows, batch sizes) need no retuning at all — clients read live config over RPC per transaction.

§ 11The nine invariants

Property tests hold all nine across random operation interleavings:

  1. Conservation. The vault balance per token equals open escrows plus unclaimed proceeds plus accrued fees, across all markets, exactly.
  2. Open interest. total_open equals the sum of live queue quantities, excluding tombstones and stale slots.
  3. Bitmap coherence, deliberately weak. Liquidity implies the bit is set, but a stale set bit over an empty level is allowed; the next fill that visits it clears it. That's what keeps cancel-to-empty O(1).
  4. Path independence. Any sequence of operations that ends in the same counters pays the same amounts.
  5. Price-time priority, scoped by start_tick. Best tick first among reachable ticks, FIFO within a level. Orders rested at better prices after simulation keep their place.
  6. Footprint discipline. No operation touches a key outside its declared family. Cap and window edges stop the loop without trapping; only a walk past pad_end traps.
  7. Bounded loops. Every loop bounds on a config constant, and route caps are shared across legs.
  8. Never crossed. No operation leaves a bid above an ask. Stopped fills refund their remainder, and post-only fails closed against the stored best.
  9. Slot validity. A queue slot is meaningful only when seq < tail_seq of the current generation. Appends leave no gaps, and stale page data can't be observed through any public path.

§ 12Deliberately not in v1

  • Global cross-market maker capital (Manifest style). It puts third-party entries back into taker footprints and reopens simulation-to-apply races. Not in v1.
  • Per-market vault sub-accounts, which would break up the shared-token serialization cluster from § 8. v2.
  • Permissionless market creation. It needs an anti-spam fee first. v2.
  • Synchronous hooks. Soroban can't put a resource cap on them, so events do the job instead.
  • Geometric ticks in the Liquidity Book style. (1+step)^id pricing changes only the id-to-price map, leaves bitmaps, levels, and settlement untouched, removes the per-market tick band, and turns a fixed pad width into a constant percentage depth. v2.
  • Pooled pro-rata levels: fungible shares per (level, generation) as a sibling market type. It would delete pages, tombstones, windows, and the per-order OrderRef whose rent is the largest cost, in exchange for giving up time priority within a level. Generation-on-sweep stays either way, because fill finality without keepers is the point. v2.
  • A volatility-scaled taker fee. The matching loop already counts crossings and the fee pot is already in the write set, so the footprint cost is zero; the price is exact fee quotes. v2.
  • Self-trade prevention, oracle-pegged orders, and batch auctions. Design notes exist, but not in v1.
